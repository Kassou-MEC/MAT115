Introduction to Trigonometry

Trigonometry starts with one question: if you know how far something has turned, where is it now? This lecture answers it in four parts, from the unit circle to graphing a wave. Use the tabs to move between parts.

Part 1

The unit circle and reference angles

Draw a circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. Start at the point (1, 0) and turn counterclockwise through an angle θ. The point where you stop has two coordinates, and those coordinates are the two main trig functions: cos θ is the x-coordinate and sin θ is the y-coordinate.

The third function is built from those two: tan θ = y ÷ x, which is the slope of the radius. This definition works for every angle, including ones past 90°, past 360°, and negative ones. The signs come free: cosine is negative on the left half of the circle and sine is negative on the bottom half.

You only need to memorize the first quadrant. The reference angle is the acute angle between the terminal side and the x-axis, and every angle has the same trig values as its reference angle, up to a plus or minus sign.

Three steps for any exact value. First, add or subtract 360° (or 2π) until the angle is between 0° and 360°. Second, find the quadrant, which gives the sign. Third, find the reference angle, which gives the number.

Part 2

Angles: degrees and radians

An angle measures how much something has turned. Start with a ray along the positive x-axis (the initial side) and rotate it about its endpoint. Where it stops is the terminal side. Counterclockwise turns are positive and clockwise turns are negative.

Degrees cut one full turn into 360 equal pieces. Radians measure the same turn with the circle's own ruler, its radius. Lay one radius along the edge of the circle, and the angle you have swept is 1 radian. The whole edge is 2π radii long, so a full turn is 2π radians.

Part 3

Right triangles and the six trig functions

In Part 1, sine and cosine were the coordinates of a point on a circle of radius 1. Drop a vertical line from that point to the x-axis and you get a right triangle with hypotenuse 1. Real triangles come in every size, so this part scales the idea up.

Take any right triangle with a 35° angle. Make it bigger or smaller and the sides change, but opposite ÷ hypotenuse stays the same number. That number depends only on the angle, so it gets a name: sin 35°. The same is true for the other pairs of sides.

The names depend on where you stand. The hypotenuse is always across from the right angle. The opposite side is across from θ, and the adjacent side touches θ.

Solving a word problem. Sketch the right triangle and mark the angle. Name the side you know and the side you want (opp, adj or hyp). Pick the function that uses exactly those two. An angle of elevation is measured up from the horizontal, and an angle of depression is measured down from it.

Part 4

Periodic functions

A function is periodic if its outputs repeat on a fixed schedule: f(x + P) = f(x) for every x. The smallest such P is the period. Tides, heartbeats, seasons and the height of a seat on a Ferris wheel all behave this way.

Sine is the model example. Follow a point around the unit circle and record only its height. After one full turn (2π) the heights start over, so sin θ has period 2π.

The height of the point, unrolled into a graph

Every sine or cosine wave has the same four features. The midline is the horizontal line halfway between the top and the bottom. The amplitude is the distance from the midline to the top. The period is the width of one full wave. The phase shift is how far the wave has slid left or right.

In y = A sin(B(x − C)) + D, each letter controls one of them. Move one slider at a time and watch which feature responds.
